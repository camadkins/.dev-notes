Before you can ask whether a machine could be conscious, you have to settle a prior question about what a mind even is. If a mind is an immaterial soul, a substance of a different kind than anything in physics, then no arrangement of silicon could ever house one and the AI-consciousness debate ends before it starts. If instead a mind is just what a certain physical system does when it is organized the right way, the door is open, because there is no longer any kind of stuff a brain has that a machine in principle could not. That prior question is the territory of physicalism, and it is the metaphysical floor the entire debate stands on, even when nobody names it.

> [!note] The idea
> **Physicalism** is the thesis that everything is physical, that there is nothing over and above the physical, and that everything supervenes on the physical. It is a metaphysical claim about what exists, a form of ontological monism, the single-substance view that reality is of one basic kind, standing opposed to dualism and idealism. Applied to the mind, it says the mind is not a separate immaterial substance but consists in the organized physical activity of the brain, with no soul required. For AI this clears away the strongest in-principle objection: if consciousness needs no immaterial ingredient, there is no metaphysical reason a non-biological physical system could not have it. What physicalism does *not* do is say *which* physical systems are conscious or *what* makes one conscious. It clears the floor and hands that question to other theories.

## The thesis that everything is physical

Physicalism is the claim that everything is physical, or as the standard formulations put it, that there is nothing over and above the physical and that everything supervenes on the physical. The supervenience version is the precise one: any world that is a physical duplicate of ours is a duplicate of ours in every respect, so you cannot change the mental, the biological, or anything else without changing something physical first. Stronger relatives of the thesis say every property is necessitated by, or grounded in, a physical property. The common spine is that the physical fixes everything else.

This is close kin to materialism, and contemporary usage often treats the two as interchangeable, though physicalism is the broader word: its "physical" covers matter along with energy, physical laws, spacetime, forces, structure, and information. It is a form of ontological monism, a single-substance account of reality, which puts it in direct opposition to dualism, which posits a second kind of mental substance, and to idealism, which makes reality arise from mind. The important thing to hold onto is that this is a thesis about what there *is*. It is metaphysics, an account of the basic furniture of the world, not yet a theory of how any particular part of that world works.

## The mind as organized physical activity

Run physicalism through the mind and you get a definite picture. The mind is not a ghost riding the body; it is something the body does. Mental states reduce to, or at least supervene on, physical states, which eliminates the need for a non-physical soul or a separate seat of consciousness. There is no extra immaterial ingredient that makes the lights come on. Whatever your experiences are, they are not floating free of your neurons. Change the physical brain and you change the mind, with no exceptions allowed, because under physicalism there is nothing else for the mind to be made of.

It is worth being careful about how tight the link to physics is. Physicalism in its non-reductive forms does not require that every mental fact be neatly *describable* in the vocabulary of physics. It requires that the mental be necessitated by the physical, fixed once the physical facts are fixed, even if the best description of a thought lives at the level of psychology rather than particle counts. The floor is ontological, not linguistic. The mind owes its existence to physical organization, whether or not physics ever gives us a satisfying sentence about any given feeling. This is the same move that the [[consciousness-access-vs-phenomenal|access-versus-phenomenal]] distinction inherits when it asks what, if anything, the felt side of experience could be over and above the brain's functional machinery.

## Why this opens the door for AI

Here is the payoff for the "Big Data and AI" ethics question. A human brain and an AI system are, at bottom, made of the same basic particles, the same electrons and protons arranged into matter. They differ in their components, neurons versus transistors, and in the kind of signal they pass, electrochemical versus electrical, but not in their fundamental constituents. If the mind is organized physical activity and nothing more, then the question of machine consciousness cannot be settled by pointing at what a brain is *made of*, because a machine is made of the same stock. The barrier that dualism would have raised, that minds require a special non-physical substance brains have and machines lack, simply is not there to raise.

So physicalism makes machine consciousness *conceivable*. It does not make it *actual*, and this is the part that gets skipped. Knowing that minds are physical tells you nothing about which physical arrangements carry experience and which are dark. Physicalism is silent on the marker. It rules out the answer "machines can't be conscious because they have no soul," and then it goes quiet, because identifying *what* makes a physical system conscious is a different project from establishing *that* consciousness is physical. The metaphysical floor is laid; the building is not designed.

## The gap physicalism leaves, and who tries to fill it

That silence is exactly the opening other theories rush to fill, and it is worth keeping their job separate from physicalism's. [[functionalism-and-multiple-realizability|Functionalism]] is a thesis not about what exists but about what *individuates* mental states: it says a mental state is defined by its functional role, its pattern of causes and effects, so that a state counts as pain because of what it does, not what it is made of. That is a different kind of claim than physicalism, and conflating them is a common error. You can be a physicalist without being a functionalist. Functionalism is one attempt to say which physical organizations have minds, namely those running the right functions, and it is the natural ally of the idea that a machine could think.

The [[scientific-theories-of-consciousness|scientific theories of consciousness]] take the empirical route to the same gap, each proposing a specific physical marker for when experience is present rather than a philosophical account of what mental states are. Both projects presuppose physicalism and then go beyond it. The live question of whether [[could-an-llm-be-conscious|an LLM could be conscious]] is downstream of all of this, an argument about whether today's systems, the products of the [[deep-learning-revolution|deep learning revolution]], satisfy whatever the correct theory turns out to require. And the [[the-biological-substrate-objection|biological substrate objection]] is the attempt to reintroduce a barrier from inside physicalism, arguing that the relevant physical fact is something about biological tissue specifically, not organization in general. Physicalism by itself does not adjudicate that fight. It only guarantees the fight is about physics, not about souls.

> [!example] What physicalism settles and what it leaves open
> 1. **Settled: no immaterial soul is required.** The mind is organized physical activity in the brain. There is no separate mental substance, so dualism's barrier to machine consciousness is removed.
> 2. **Settled: same basic stuff.** Brain and machine are both arrangements of the same particles, differing in components and signal type, not in fundamental constituents. "It's only made of transistors" is not an argument.
> 3. **Left open: which physical systems are conscious.** Physicalism names no marker. A rock, a thermostat, a brain, and a server are all physical; the thesis does not tell you which, if any, feel.
> 4. **Left open: what makes a system conscious.** This is handed to functionalism (the right function), the scientific theories (a specific physical signature), or the substrate objection (biology specifically). Physicalism guarantees the answer is physical without supplying it.
