The central object of computer science, the algorithm, is named after a person. Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi was a mathematician who worked in Baghdad around the year 820, and both the word "algorithm" and the word "algebra" trace back to him.

> [!note] The idea
> An algorithm is a finite, systematic, step-by-step procedure that solves every instance of a class of problems, rather than one case at a time. Al-Khwarizmi wrote such procedures down, and his name became the word for them.

## The House of Wisdom

Al-Khwarizmi, of Persian stock, worked during the Islamic Golden Age at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad around 820, a center of translation and scholarship. His mathematics drew together [[cs/dsa/euclidean-algorithms|Greek, Indian, and earlier traditions]] and pushed them forward.

## Two words from one man

His name, Latinized as Algoritmi, became the English words algorism and algorithm. The short title of his treatise, al-jabr, became the word algebra. Few individuals are the etymological root of two pillars of a field.

## Step by step

What earns him the connection to computing is method. His systematic approach to solving linear and quadratic equations gave a general recipe: [[cs/dsa/algorithm-efficiency|a sequence of steps that works for every problem of a given form]], rather than a clever trick for one case. That generality is exactly what an algorithm is.

## Forward

The formal question of what a step-by-step procedure can and cannot compute waited a thousand years, until [[turing-and-computability|Turing and the theory of computability]] gave "algorithm" a precise mathematical meaning.
